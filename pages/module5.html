5. System design

50 min Requires Module 4 Active

The four components of a solar system

A complete solar energy system has four main subsystems that work together: the PV array (panels), a charge controller, a battery bank (for off-grid or hybrid systems), and an inverter. In a grid-tie system without batteries, the charge controller is replaced by the inverter's built-in MPPT, and surplus energy is exported to the grid rather than stored.

Understanding how these components interact — and how to size each one correctly — is the core skill of solar system design. Undersizing any component creates a bottleneck; oversizing wastes money. The goal is a balanced system that meets the load reliably at the lowest possible cost.

💡 Design order matters. Always start with the load (how much energy you need), then size the battery bank, then the array, and finally the charge controller and wiring. Working backwards from the load prevents costly mistakes.

Step 1 — Calculate the daily load

The first step is to list every electrical load, its power rating and how many hours per day it runs. Multiplying power (W) by time (h) gives energy in watt-hours (Wh). Summing all loads gives the daily energy demand.

Add a 20–25 % margin for inverter losses, wiring losses and battery inefficiency. The result is the system's daily design load.

🔢 Example. Fridge 150 W × 24 h = 3 600 Wh; lights 60 W × 5 h = 300 Wh; TV 80 W × 4 h = 320 Wh; phone 10 W × 2 h = 20 Wh. Total = 4 240 Wh/day. With 20 % margin → design load ≈ 5 088 Wh/day.

Step 2 — Size the battery bank

The battery bank stores energy to cover night-time consumption and cloudy days. Two key parameters drive battery sizing: autonomy (how many days without sun the system must survive) and depth of discharge (DoD — how deeply the battery can be drained without damage).

Lead-acid batteries are limited to 50 % DoD to protect lifespan; lithium (LFP) batteries tolerate 80–90 % DoD. A larger DoD means you need fewer kWh of nominal capacity to deliver the same usable energy.

🔢 Example. 2 days autonomy, 50 % DoD: C = 5 088 × 2 / 0.5 = 20 352 Wh ≈ 20.4 kWh nominal.

Step 3 — Size the solar array

The array must replenish the battery bank in the available peak sun hours (PSH). PSH is the number of hours per day when irradiance averages 1 000 W/m² — it varies by location and season. Use the worst-month PSH value for a reliable year-round design.

Array power (Wp) = Daily design load / (PSH × system efficiency). System efficiency accounts for wiring losses (~3 %), battery charge/discharge (~85 %) and inverter conversion (~95 %). Combined efficiency ≈ 75–80 %.

🔢 Example. PSH = 4.5 h/day. Array Wp = 5 088 / (4.5 × 0.78) ≈ 1 450 Wp. With 400 Wp panels: 1 450 / 400 = 3.6 → round up to 4 panels = 1 600 Wp.

Step 4 — Choose the charge controller

The charge controller sits between the array and the battery bank, preventing overcharge and over-discharge. There are two main types: PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) and MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking).

PWM Pulse Width Modulation

Simple, reliable and low-cost. Works efficiently only when panel voltage closely matches battery voltage. Best for small or very budget-constrained systems.

MPPT Maximum Power Point Tracking

Harvests 20–30 % more energy by continuously optimising the panel's operating point. Handles high-voltage string inputs and steps voltage down for the battery. Recommended for systems with more than 1–2 panels.

PWM controllers are simple and affordable but only work efficiently when the panel voltage closely matches the battery voltage. MPPT controllers harvest 20–30 % more energy by continuously finding the panel's maximum power point. For any system with more than 1–2 panels, MPPT is almost always the better investment.

⚡ Sizing the controller. Controller current (A) ≥ Array Isc × 1.25. Example: 4 panels in parallel, each Isc = 10 A → 40 A × 1.25 = 50 A controller minimum.

Putting it together — system wiring overview

A typical off-grid residential system connects as follows: the PV array feeds into the charge controller, which charges the battery bank at the correct voltage. The battery bank powers the inverter, which outputs 120 V or 230 V AC for the home's distribution panel. DC loads (12 V or 24 V) can connect directly to the battery through a fuse.

Cable sizing is critical at every stage. Use the formula Vdrop = I × Rcable and target less than 3 % voltage drop on each run. Larger cable cross-sections cost more upfront but pay back in reduced losses over the system's 25-year life.

⚠️ Safety first. Every DC circuit requires an appropriately rated fuse or breaker as close to the battery positive terminal as possible. A short circuit in an unprotected battery circuit can produce currents of thousands of amperes, causing fires within seconds.

Key terms

PSH Peak Sun Hours — equivalent hours per day of full 1 000 W/m² irradiance. Used to size the array.
DoD Depth of Discharge — percentage of battery capacity drawn before recharging. Higher DoD shortens battery life.
Autonomy Number of days the battery bank can power the load without any solar input.
Charge controller Device that manages current flow from the array to the battery, preventing overcharge and over-discharge.
MPPT Maximum Power Point Tracking — advanced charge controller algorithm that maximises energy harvest from the array.
System efficiency Combined efficiency of wiring, battery, and inverter losses — typically 75–80 % for off-grid systems.
