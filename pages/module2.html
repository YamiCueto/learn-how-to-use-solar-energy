2. How panels work

40 min Requires Module 1 Active

The photovoltaic cell

A solar panel is built from many small photovoltaic (PV) cells. Each cell is a thin slice of semiconductor material — most commonly crystalline silicon — treated to have two distinct layers: a negatively-charged n-type layer on top and a positively-charged p-type layer below. The interface between these layers is called the p-n junction.

When a photon of light strikes the cell with enough energy, it knocks an electron loose from the silicon lattice. The built-in electric field at the p-n junction pushes the free electron toward the n-type side and the corresponding "hole" toward the p-type side. This charge separation creates a voltage — typically around 0.5–0.6 V per cell — that drives current through an external circuit.

Step-by-step: light to electricity

1
Photon absorption

A photon from the sun strikes the silicon surface. If its energy exceeds the bandgap (~1.1 eV for silicon), it excites an electron to the conduction band.

2
Charge separation

The built-in electric field at the p-n junction sweeps the free electron to the n-side and the hole to the p-side, preventing immediate recombination.

3
Current collection

Metal contacts on the front (thin grid lines) and rear (full sheet) collect the electrons. The front grid is designed to be as thin as possible to minimise shading.

4
External circuit

Electrons flow out through the front contacts, through your load (lights, motors, etc.) and back in through the rear contact — that's usable DC electricity.

5
From cells to modules

Individual cells (~156 × 156 mm) are wired in series strings, encapsulated in EVA polymer, sandwiched between tempered glass and a backsheet, and framed in aluminium — that's the panel you install on a roof.

Types of solar panels

Not all solar panels are the same. The three main commercial technologies differ in efficiency, cost, appearance and best use case.

◆ Monocrystalline

Mono-Si

Efficiency 20–24 %
Relative cost Higher
Lifespan 25–30 yr

Best efficiency, uniform black look, ideal for limited roof space.

◈ Polycrystalline

Poly-Si

Efficiency 15–18 %
Relative cost Medium
Lifespan 20–25 yr

Blue speckled look, lower cost, slightly lower efficiency than mono.

▣ Thin-film

CdTe / CIS / a-Si

Efficiency 10–13 %
Relative cost Lower
Lifespan 10–20 yr

Flexible, performs better in high heat & low light, lower efficiency.

Why real output is lower than the rating

The nameplate wattage printed on a panel assumes Standard Test Conditions (STC). In practice, several factors reduce output. Understanding them helps you design a system that actually meets your energy needs.

🌡️ Temperature coefficient. Silicon panels lose roughly 0.35–0.5 % of power per °C above 25 °C. On a hot roof where cell temperature reaches 65 °C, output drops by ~14–20 % compared to the STC rating.
Factor Typical loss Notes
Temperature 10–20 % Higher cell temp → lower Voc and power
Shading 0–50 %+ Even partial shading on one cell affects whole string
Soiling / dust 1–25 % Worse in arid climates; regular cleaning helps
Wiring losses 1–3 % Use correct cable gauge to minimise resistance
Inverter losses 2–5 % Modern inverters are 95–98 % efficient
Panel degradation 0.5 % / yr Panels lose ~0.5 % output per year (LID + aging)

Key electrical parameters on the datasheet

Every panel ships with a datasheet. Knowing what each parameter means lets you size wiring, inverters and charge controllers correctly.

  • Pmax (Wp) — Peak power at STC. Example: 400 Wp.
  • Voc — Open-circuit voltage. The voltage with no load connected. Used to size inverter input and avoid over-voltage.
  • Vmp — Voltage at maximum power point. Typically ~80 % of Voc. This is the operating voltage for maximum output.
  • Isc — Short-circuit current. The maximum current the panel can produce. Used to size fuses and wiring.
  • Imp — Current at maximum power point. Combined with Vmp: Pmax = Vmp × Imp.
  • η (eta) — Efficiency. Ratio of electrical output to incident solar power × panel area.
💡 Design tip. Always use Voc (not Vmp) when checking the maximum input voltage of your inverter or charge controller — it's the highest voltage the panel will ever produce.
