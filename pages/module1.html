1. Intro to solar energy

35 min No prerequisites Active

Why solar energy?

The sun delivers more energy to Earth in one hour than all of humanity consumes in an entire year. Capturing even a tiny fraction of that energy can power homes, businesses and entire cities — without burning a single gram of fossil fuel.

Solar photovoltaic (PV) systems convert sunlight directly into electricity using semiconducting materials. They have no moving parts, produce zero emissions during operation, and their cost has dropped more than 90 % over the last decade, making them the cheapest source of electricity in history for most of the world.

💡 Key insight. In 2024, solar PV generated over 6 % of global electricity — up from less than 0.1 % in 2010. The energy transition is already underway.

How the sun delivers energy

The sun produces energy through nuclear fusion — hydrogen atoms merge to form helium, releasing enormous amounts of energy as electromagnetic radiation. That radiation travels 150 million km to reach Earth in about 8 minutes.

At the top of Earth's atmosphere, the solar irradiance is approximately 1 361 W/m² (the "solar constant"). By the time sunlight reaches sea level — after being absorbed and scattered by the atmosphere — a clear-sky peak value of about 1 000 W/m² is the standard used in panel ratings.

📐 Standard Test Conditions (STC). Panel manufacturers rate their products at 1 000 W/m² irradiance, 25 °C cell temperature and AM 1.5 spectrum. Real-world output is almost always lower.

The solar resource: irradiance & insolation

Two measurements describe how much solar energy is available at a site:

  • Irradiance (W/m²) — the instantaneous power of sunlight falling on a surface. Varies with time of day, season and weather.
  • Insolation (kWh/m²/day) — the total energy received over a full day. Also called "peak sun hours". Typical values: 3–7 kWh/m²/day depending on location.
  • Tilt & orientation — panels facing the equator at an angle roughly equal to local latitude receive the most annual energy.

Photovoltaic conversion basics

A photovoltaic cell is a semiconductor device — usually made of silicon — that produces a small direct current (DC) voltage when photons of light knock electrons loose from their atoms. This is the photoelectric effect, first explained by Einstein in 1905 (for which he won the Nobel Prize).

  • A single silicon cell produces ~0.5 V DC.
  • Cells are wired in series inside a module (panel) to reach useful voltages (12 V, 24 V, 48 V…).
  • Modules are grouped into strings and combined into an array to meet a system's power requirements.
  • An inverter converts the DC output into AC electricity for use in homes and the grid.

A brief history of solar PV

1839

Photovoltaic effect discovered

Edmond Becquerel observes that certain materials produce electricity when exposed to light.

1954

First practical solar cell

Bell Labs creates a silicon PV cell with ~6 % efficiency — enough to power small electronics.

1973

Oil crisis spurs research

Rising oil prices push governments to invest in renewable energy research, including solar.

2000s

Grid-scale solar takes off

Feed-in tariffs in Germany and Spain trigger the first large-scale solar boom in Europe.

2024

Solar is the cheapest electricity source

New utility-scale solar achieves costs below $0.02/kWh in many regions — beating all other generation sources.

Key terms

Photon A particle of light that carries energy proportional to its frequency.
PV cell A semiconductor device that converts photons directly into DC electricity.
Irradiance Instantaneous solar power per unit area, measured in W/m².
Insolation Total solar energy received per unit area over a day, in kWh/m².
STC Standard Test Conditions — 1 000 W/m², 25 °C, AM 1.5 spectrum used for panel ratings.
Inverter Device that converts DC electricity from panels/batteries into AC electricity for loads.
